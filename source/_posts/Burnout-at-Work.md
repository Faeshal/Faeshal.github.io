I haven't posted in a long time, and I think it's only fair to explain why: I went through a period of genuine burnout. Not the kind a long weekend can fix, but the kind that quietly wears you down over months.

## My Story

It started when I joined a project already in the middle of its development cycle, rather than from day one. By the time I came on board, things were already unraveling: the codebase was in poor shape, timelines had slipped repeatedly, and yet the expectations placed on me were disproportionately high, as though my presence alone was meant to turn the project around overnight.

Compounding this, communication with our client at the time was far from smooth. Requirements shifted frequently, feedback was often vague or inconsistent, and every misunderstanding translated into additional pressure on our side. Caught between a project that was already in crisis, expectations I had little realistic chance of meeting, and a client relationship that felt like an uphill battle, I found myself mentally and physically drained on an almost daily basis.

If there's one lesson I took away from that period, it's this: **open communication with your manager matters more than almost anything else when you're heading toward burnout.** I was fortunate in that regard. My manager at the time was professional and genuinely empathetic. When I was honest about how much I was struggling, he didn't dismiss it, instead choosing to listen, recalibrate expectations where possible, and give me the space I needed. That support made a far greater difference than any productivity technique could have.

Eventually, the project came to a close, and I was able to step away from what I can only describe, only half-jokingly, as a "hell project." Looking back, I'd like to share what I went through and what genuinely helped, in case you find yourself in a similar situation today.

## What Burnout Actually Is

Burnout isn't just "being tired." Being tired is what a good night's sleep fixes. Burnout is a state of emotional, physical, and mental exhaustion caused by prolonged, excessive stress, and it doesn't go away with a weekend off. The World Health Organization even classifies it as an occupational phenomenon, not a medical condition, but that doesn't make it any less real.

Some signs I noticed in myself during that period:

- Dreading Monday from the moment Sunday afternoon starts
- Feeling cynical or detached about work you used to care about
- Struggling to focus even on simple tasks
- Getting irritated over small, unimportant things
- Physical symptoms: headaches, poor sleep, constant fatigue
- A creeping feeling that nothing you do actually matters

## What Causes It

From my own experience, burnout usually isn't caused by one big thing. It's death by a thousand cuts:

1. **Chronic overload** — constantly being asked to do more with the same 24 hours, sprint after sprint, with no room to breathe.
2. **Lack of control** — having no real say in your workload, priorities, or how you do your job.
3. **Unclear expectations** — not knowing what "done" or "good enough" actually looks like, so you keep overworking just in case.
4. **Poor recognition** — putting in real effort and having it go unnoticed, or worse, taken for granted.
5. **Broken communication** — unclear or contradictory feedback, from a client or otherwise, that turns every interaction into extra pressure.
6. **Values mismatch** — when what the company asks you to do conflicts with what you actually care about.

Notice none of these are really about "not being resilient enough." Burnout is mostly an environment problem wearing a personal problem costume.

## How to Deal With It

There's no magic fix, but here's what genuinely helped me:

- **Talk to your manager, honestly and early.** This is the single most important thing on this list. If you have a manager who's willing to listen, use that. Don't wait until you're completely empty to speak up.
- **Name it early.** The moment I noticed I was dreading work for no clear reason, I stopped and asked myself why, instead of pushing through and pretending it was fine.
- **Protect actual recovery time.** Not "I'll relax after I finish this one more thing," but a real, scheduled block of time where work is simply not allowed to show up.
- **Fix what's actually fixable.** If it's workload, negotiate it. If it's unclear expectations, ask for clarity. If it's a difficult client, push for clearer communication channels instead of absorbing the chaos silently.
- **Reconnect with the "why."** Sometimes you just need to remember why you got into this field in the first place, and whether your current role still lines up with that.
- **Know when to walk away.** Sometimes the healthiest option isn't a better routine, it's getting off that project, that team, or that company altogether. There's no shame in that.

## The One Thing I'm Most Grateful For

Here's the part I really want you to take away from this post: that horrible period of burnout is the reason I picked up cycling, a hobby I still stick to today.

I didn't start cycling to get fit or build discipline or any of that. Honestly, none of that crossed my mind. I started purely because I needed a way to release the stress that was crushing me. Riding alone, no destination in mind, just me, the road, and the sound of my own breathing, became my healing process. It still is, to this day.

So if you're going through burnout right now, here's my honest advice: find a hobby, any hobby. If you can, make it something physical, some form of exercise, because letting the stress out of your body first makes a real difference. Don't try to solve the actual problem while you're still drowning in stress. Let the stress come down first. Then, slowly, calmly, think through what's actually wrong and what you can do about it.

You'll get through it. I did, and cycling is still with me to remind me how I got out.
